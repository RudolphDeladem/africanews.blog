Ghanaian-built Simscuit puts a whole electronics lab in a browser tab

Four weeks after its first release, the platform lets anyone wire a circuit, run Arduino code, design a circuit board, test a robot and order the parts in cedis, without buying a kit first.

By the africanews.blog tech desk · 16 November 2026 · 8 min read

Anyone who has tried to learn electronics in Ghana knows the order of events. First you find the money for an Arduino, a breadboard and a bag of sensors. Then you wire something the wrong way round, a part gets hot, and the lesson ends.

Simscuit, a platform built by a team of Ghanaian founders, wants to reverse that order. You build the circuit on screen, run the real code on it, watch it work or fail, and only then buy what the project needs. Its first version went live on 19 October 2026. By 9 November it had shipped twelve updates, paid plans and a marketplace priced in Ghana cedis.

What Simscuit is

At its simplest, Simscuit is a virtual electronics bench. Its core tool, Circuit Studio, gives you an Arduino Uno, a breadboard and a library of parts. You join them pin to pin, write a sketch and press run. LEDs light up, servos sweep and buzzers sound, on screen.

Around that core the team has built a set of connected tools that share one project and one account. The company counts twelve of them, covering circuit boards, 3D design, robotics, phone apps, courses and a parts shop. Its pitch is that a student should not need a circuit program, a board design program, a CAD package, a code editor and a powerful computer to take one idea from sketch to finished object.

The company says the platform holds more than 1,000 parts, each with ratings and pin details taken from its datasheet and linked to the source. That matters for how the simulator behaves: parts heat up and burn out when pushed past their real limits. An LED without a resistor dies on screen, the simulator tells you why, and you undo and try again at no cost.

The problems it is aimed at

On its About page, Simscuit lists seven things it says stop people building electronics. Parts cost money before you know what you need. One wrong wire destroys a component. The software is scattered and expensive. Schools do not have the labs. Lessons explain theory and leave the building to you. A working prototype rarely becomes a finished product. And there is nobody to ask at midnight.

Those problems exist everywhere, but they bite hardest where lab benches are few and imported components are costly. A class that shares a handful of kits tends to watch a demonstration at the front of the room. Simscuit's answer is to give every student a full lab on the laptop, Chromebook or phone they already have.

Everything you can do on Simscuit

Design and simulate circuits

Wire real parts on a breadboard and run Arduino code against them in Circuit Studio. You can switch to a 3D view of the build, render a studio-lit image of it, and export a project as code, a bill of materials or a PDF report.

Turn a circuit into a circuit board

PCB Design Studio converts a schematic into a two-layer board. You place parts by hand or automatically, route the copper tracks, run design rule checks, view the board in 3D and export the Gerber, drill, parts list and placement files a factory needs.

Model and print the case

3D Design Studio is parametric CAD in the browser. You sketch, extrude, cut and fillet enclosures and brackets, assemble parts, produce engineering drawings and export 3MF, STL or OpenSCAD files for a 3D printer.

Build and test robots

Robotics Studio starts from sixteen standard platforms, among them wheeled rovers, a quadruped, a hexapod, a humanoid, robot arms, drones and an underwater vehicle. You fit motors and sensors, wire them, write the firmware in Arduino code and run the robot on Rapier and MuJoCo physics. You can push it, load it or inject a sensor fault and chart how it responds.

Program real hardware

Hardware Programmer is a code editor for Arduino, ESP32, Raspberry Pi Pico and STM32 boards. It checks sketches for common mistakes and reads a connected board over USB through a serial monitor.

Give your gadget a phone app

App Builder lets you drag buttons, sliders, gauges and charts into an app that talks to your board over Bluetooth or Wi-Fi. You can download it as an Android Studio project or publish it as a live web app.

Plan a project from an idea

Describe what you want to make in words, a voice note or pictures, and Project Builder returns a plan: the parts and their shop prices, the total cost, the wiring, the code and the build steps.

Learn, with certificates

Tutorials open a working circuit you can change. Two free courses, Python for Beginners and C++ for Beginners, run real code in the browser and end in a certificate. Games and quizzes drill the basics, such as reading resistor colour bands and Ohm's law.

Look inside machines, and more

Explorer is a 3D atlas of 122 machines, tools and components you can take apart and be quizzed on. It has grown beyond engineering to include an anatomy lab, live science simulations, and sections for biology, chemistry, physics, earth and space, and computer science. It needs no account.

Buy the parts, in cedis

The Marketplace lists 307 components, from resistors to Raspberry Pi boards, with prices in Ghana cedis. An Arduino Uno R3 is listed at GH₵350. Ready-made kits match the platform's template projects, so the LED Blink kit, at GH₵398, contains exactly what the simulated version used.

Ask The Bee

The Bee is Simscuit's AI assistant, available on every page. On the free plan it gives short answers. On paid plans it explains and troubleshoots, and on Pro it reads your circuit, writes code and builds circuits, boards and 3D shapes from a description.

Join The Hive

The Hive is the community. Members get a bee identity, share builds with photos, join groups, earn ranks from Hatchling to Hive Legend, and use Bee Match to find people to build with.

Run a whole school on it

Organisations give a school, university or company its own workspace. An administrator can create hundreds of student accounts from one spreadsheet, choose which tools are switched on, share projects and see usage figures. Younger students sign in with a username and a six-digit PIN, with no email address needed, and the public community can be kept closed.

Earn as a creator

A Creator Programme has two tracks. Video makers are paid per 1,000 verified views on campaign content. Teachers and engineers can propose courses, which Simscuit produces and publishes with them for a share of sales. Applications are open, though the company says no paid campaign is running yet.

Paying for six months saves 10 per cent, and a yearly plan gives two months free. If a paid plan ends, the company says nothing is deleted: cloud projects stay readable and downloadable.

Where it runs

Simscuit opens in Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox with nothing to install. It can also be installed from the browser on phones, tablets and computers, and there are desktop downloads for Windows and Linux. Installed versions keep opening pages you have already used when the connection drops, a useful trait where data is expensive and networks are patchy. App Store, Google Play, Microsoft Store and macOS versions are marked as coming soon.

What is not ready yet

The company is unusually direct about what is unfinished, labelling features "Soon" or "Planned" across its site. You cannot yet compile and upload code to a real board from the browser. Certificate courses in electronics, Arduino and robotics have not opened. A shelf of calculators and converters is still being built. Classes, assignments and single sign-on for schools are planned. Some simulated parts are marked "Visual only", meaning they appear on the board but do not yet behave electrically.

Simscuit is also a month old. Its claims about simulation accuracy have not been independently tested, and the company has not published user numbers. Its website does not name the people behind it.

Why it matters

Browser circuit simulators are not new. What is different here is the scope, and where it comes from. Simscuit joins the simulator to the parts shop, prices that shop in cedis, accepts mobile money and designs its school accounts around classrooms where children do not have email addresses. Those are choices made by people who know the market they are building for.

Whether a young platform can keep twelve tools working well at once is the open question. The team's pace so far, twelve updates in three weeks, suggests it intends to try.
