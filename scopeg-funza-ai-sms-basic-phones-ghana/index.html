Scopeg AI wants to put an AI assistant on every basic phone in Ghana, by text message

Funza AI answers questions by ordinary SMS, with no app, no smartphone and no data bundle. A partnership with Ghana's youth ministry is meant to take it national this month.

By the africanews.blog tech desk · 16 November 2026 · 4 min read

Most of the excitement about artificial intelligence assumes a smartphone and a data connection. A young Ghanaian company is betting on the phone most people already have, and the oldest feature on it.

Scopeg AI has partnered with Ghana's Ministry of Youth Development and Empowerment to roll out Funza AI, a service that answers questions by text message, Business Tech Africa reported on 16 November 2026. The company plans to launch it nationally by the end of November.

How it works

You send a question as an ordinary text message, and an AI-generated answer comes back by SMS within seconds, according to the report. There is no app to download, and you do not need Wi-Fi or a data bundle. That means it works on the cheap feature phones that are still common in Ghana, not only on smartphones.

The SMS short code that users will text has not been made public yet, and neither has the standard price. People who join through the ministry's youth programmes will get discounted access and a limited number of free questions each day.

Who it is for

Scopeg describes four main groups of users: students who want help with schoolwork, farmers looking for agricultural information, owners of small businesses, and young jobseekers who want career advice.

The gap it is aimed at is large. More than nine million people in Ghana, roughly a quarter of the population, do not use the internet, according to figures cited in the partnership announcement. For them, AI chat tools are out of reach.

The mobile money comparison

Tamakloe, the chief executive, said Funza was built to spread AI the way mobile money spread financial services: by reaching people on basic phones rather than waiting for them to get smartphones. Mobile money transformed how Ghanaians pay and save precisely because it worked over simple menus on any handset.

What the ministry brings

For Scopeg, the partnership offers a route to young people through government youth programmes, instead of having to find users one by one. The youth minister, George Opare Addo, said the ministry would support youth-led Ghanaian businesses that build products for local problems. The report does not say whether the ministry is paying for the service.

What we do not know yet

A good deal. The report does not say which languages Funza understands, which AI model it runs on, how accurate its answers are, or how the company will stop a short text reply from giving wrong medical or farming advice. It also does not mention investors or funding. The business depends on turning subsidised users into paying ones.

SMS has hard limits. A text message holds 160 characters, so long explanations must be split across several messages, each of which can carry a cost.

Not the only one

Scopeg is part of a growing group of Ghanaian companies building AI for people who are offline or on basic phones. Kola Market, which serves customers through WhatsApp, SMS and voice in local languages, was recently named a grantee of the GSMA AI Innovation Fund, according to a GhScientific news round-up. Scopeg also makes Carsa, a translation tool for African languages by text and voice.

Why it matters

The next wave of AI users in Africa is unlikely to arrive through a premium smartphone app. If Funza works at the scale it promises, it would show that useful AI can travel over the same plain networks that carried mobile money. The launch date is close, and the details still to come, such as price, short code and languages, will decide how many people actually use it.

This article is based on Business Tech Africa's report of 16 November 2026 and a GhScientific round-up published the same week. Funza AI had not launched nationally at the time of writing, and details may change.
