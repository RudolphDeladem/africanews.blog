Ghana's 2027 budget promises a National AI Centre and US$300 million for tech

Finance Minister Cassiel Ato Forson made technology one of eight pillars of what he calls a new economy. Here is what the plan says about AI, connectivity, skills and science, and what it does not say.

By the africanews.blog tech desk · 16 November 2026 · 6 min read

When Ghana's Finance Minister, Dr Cassiel Ato Forson, presented the 2027 budget to Parliament on 12 November 2026, technology did not get a speech of its own. It got something more useful: a line in the plan, a sum of money and a named institution.

The budget sets aside US$300 million over 2027 and 2028 for digital infrastructure, artificial intelligence and tech jobs, according to MyJoyOnline. At the centre of it is a new National Artificial Intelligence Centre.

The bigger plan

The budget frames its spending as the first year of a US$10 billion, four-year programme running from 2027 to 2030, with GH¢30 billion committed in 2027. The programme has eight pillars. One of them is a "technology economy", alongside energy, agriculture and others, according to Citi Newsroom.

Total spending is GH¢389.2 billion, of which GH¢100.4 billion is capital spending on things that last, such as roads, power plants and buildings, Graphic Online reported. The minister called it "a decisive shift towards productive expenditure".

A National AI Centre

The centrepiece of the technology pillar is a National Artificial Intelligence Centre. As described in the budget coverage, it would provide:

  • high-performance computing, the powerful machines needed to train and run AI models
  • secure environments for handling data
  • research labs and facilities to test AI models
  • professional training
  • support for tech start-ups

The budget names the areas where it wants AI used: agriculture, healthcare, energy, mining, logistics, public services and Ghanaian languages. It also promises "responsible AI" safeguards covering privacy, cybersecurity and accountability.

The coverage does not say where the centre will be built, when it will open, who will run it or how much of the US$300 million it will receive.

From 2G to 4G in rural areas

The same pillar commits to upgrading rural areas still served by old 2G networks to reliable 4G. A 2G signal is enough for calls and text messages but too slow for most of the modern internet, including video lessons, online banking apps and AI tools. "Rural connectivity must allow every young person to contribute to the development of the economy," the budget presentation said, according to the Ghana News Agency's live coverage.

Skills

The plan includes training for young people in software development, data analysis, AI, computer networks and digital business services, plus help for small businesses to adopt digital tools. The coverage we read did not give a number of people to be trained or a separate budget line for the government's existing One Million Coders programme.

2,000 nuclear scientists

The budget's most striking science target is in energy. It plans to train more than 2,000 scientists for nuclear power, a figure reported by Citi Newsroom, MyJoyOnline and the Ghana News Agency. A US$200 million nuclear readiness programme is to run from January 2027 to December 2028. Citi Newsroom reports that the aim is for Ghana to be ready to sign a contract for a state-owned nuclear power plant by December 2028.

For the solar industry, the budget removes import duty and VAT on solar panels and batteries.

Taxes

The minister said no new taxes would be introduced. Revenue is instead meant to rise through better tax compliance, closing leaks and what Citi Newsroom describes as "digital economy taxation". The coverage did not explain what that phrase covers, so it is not yet clear whether it affects online services, digital platforms or mobile money.

Citi Newsroom also reports that money raised by the Communications Service Tax, a levy on telecom services, will no longer be capped for youth programmes, with GH¢1 billion released straight away.

What is missing

The reports we read did not mention any change to the e-levy on electronic transfers, a dedicated fund for start-ups, a national data centre or broadband plans beyond the rural 4G upgrade. Those may appear in the full budget statement, which we were not able to review.

The opposition was not impressed. The Minority in Parliament accused the government of misleading Ghanaians with its "no new taxes" claim, MyJoyOnline reported, and Minority Leader Alexander Afenyo-Markin dismissed the budget as "another round of slogans and no jobs".

Why it matters

Many African governments have published AI strategies. Fewer have put money and an institution behind them. A national computing centre matters because training and running AI models needs expensive hardware that most Ghanaian start-ups and universities cannot afford alone. If the centre is built and opened to them, it could change what local researchers can attempt, especially for Ghanaian languages, which global tech companies have largely ignored.

The test will be in the detail the budget does not yet give: a site, a date, a budget for the centre itself, and rules on who gets to use it.

This article is based on reporting of the 2027 Budget Statement by MyJoyOnline, Citi Newsroom, Graphic Online and the Ghana News Agency, published 12–13 November 2026. We could not review the full budget document, and figures may be revised as Parliament debates it.
